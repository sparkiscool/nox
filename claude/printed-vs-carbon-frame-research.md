# Carbon plate still beats printed frames

Don't print the frame. For a 400 to 520 g caged 3.5 inch quad on 2004 motors, a single carbon plate is the lightest proven motor platform, and the best move is a custom-cut 2.5 to 3 mm plate at about 17 to 20 g (estimate) to replace the cut-down Bee35 plate, which is probably 24 to 32 g (estimate, nobody publishes it, so weigh it first). A full printed frame in anything the A1 mini prints reliably lands around 35 to 50 g (estimate) and adds a heat problem at the motor mounts. A printed-centre plus carbon-rod hybrid is roughly a wash against a thin plate on frame mass alone (about 18 to 25 g, my estimate, no weighed example exists at this size). It only wins if the same rods also do the cage's job, and nobody has published a stiff rod-truss motor frame at this scale. The honest size of the prize: swapping plates saves roughly 4 to 12 g (estimate), 1 to 3% of all-up weight. The frame is already about 5% of the craft, so the bigger grams are in the cage and in merging cage with frame. No measured printed-vs-carbon flight vibration comparison exists for any small quad, so the stiffness argument below rests on data sheets and beam calculations, not flight logs.

## The lightest printed frames weigh 27 to 35 g and carry half this load

Published printed 3 to 3.5 inch frames cluster at **27 to 35 g** for the light ones and 45 to 55 g for the conservative solid ones. Project Synthara is about 29 g in PC-CF ([Printables](https://www.printables.com/model/1216454-project-synthara-generative-design-fpv-drone-frame)), ManaFly 3.5 is 27 to 35 g depending on material ([MakerWorld](https://makerworld.com/models/2512262)), and a solid 3 inch deadcat is 45 to 55 g ([MakerWorld](https://makerworld.com/models/2727274)). All of these are designer-stated, none independently weighed, and every one carries 1404 motors at about 250 g all-up. No published printed frame for 2004 motors at 400 g was found. Scaling to your craft, the researchers put a printed body plus arms at **35 to 50 g (estimate)**, excluding cage.

Two design schools exist. The light one is a hollow shell: 2 to 3 walls, 0 to 3% infill, printed flat, arms taller than wide. The Crab 4 inch hits 25 to 30 g this way ([MakerWorld](https://makerworld.com/models/1284211)). The heavy one is generative organic shapes at 6 walls and 100% infill. Generative design is not buying mass here: hobby generative frames are no lighter than hand-drawn hollow shells, and the one bench-tested generative frame found failed two of three load cases ([TMU thesis](https://rshare.library.torontomu.ca/articles/thesis/Generative_Design_for_3D_Printing_of_Advanced_Aerial_Drones/23330861/1/files/41107547.pdf)).

The good flight reports at low mass are all in carbon-filled engineering filament (PET-CF, PA-CF, PC-CF). The bad ones (flex, resonance, motor shutdowns, "night and day" against carbon) are solid PLA and PETG ([Adrelien](https://adrelien.com/3d-printed-drone-frame/), [IntoFPV](https://intofpv.com/printthread.php?tid=22141)). One printed 4 inch unibody author reports heat under the motors softening the plastic and loosening screws ([MakerWorld](https://www.makerworld.com/en/models/541413)).

## The A1 mini limits you to the two weakest fibre filaments

Problem: the filaments that make light printed frames work are off the table. Bambu's guide allows **PLA-CF and PETG-CF** on open-frame printers (PETG-CF with a warning about weak layer bonding) and marks PA6-CF, PAHT-CF, PET-CF and PPA-CF as not recommended ([Bambu wiki](https://wiki.bambulab.com/en/general/filament-guide-material-table)). The A1 mini spec sheet is stricter still and lists all carbon or glass filled polymers as not recommended, so Bambu's two documents disagree ([A1 mini spec sheet](https://www.3dprintergear.com.au/assets/files/A1%20Mini%20TDS.pdf)). Either way you need a hardened nozzle. Polymaker's Fiberon PA6-CF20 and PET-CF17 fit the machine's limits on paper (40 to 80 C bed, room-temperature chamber) but run the hotend at its 300 C ceiling and assume a 100 to 120 C anneal; no test of them on an A1 mini was found ([Fiberon PA6-CF20 TDS](https://fiberon.polymaker.com/wp-content/uploads/TDS_FIBERON-PA6-CF20_V1.1_EN.pdf)).

PLA-CF is the stiffest thing you can print reliably at 3.7 to 3.95 GPa in-plane, against roughly **45 GPa for woven carbon plate** ([Bambu PLA-CF TDS](https://www.additive-x.com/shop/mpattachments/file/viewonline/id/817/product_id/2578), [Rock West](https://www.rockwestcomposites.com/204-22.html)). Across layers it drops to about 2.3 GPa and half the strength. It also softens at 55 C, the same as plain PLA; fibre does not raise that. PETG-CF gives 74 C but is about 25% less stiff. No source gives the motor-base temperature of a 2004 hovering a 400 g quad, so whether PLA-CF survives at the motor mount is an open question you have to measure.

Shape rescues part of this. By the researchers' own beam calculation, a solid PLA-CF arm matching a carbon plate arm in bending is 2.25 times as thick and 1.8 times as heavy, but a hollow PLA-CF box with 0.8 mm walls matches a 3 x 12 mm carbon arm at about 0.67 of its mass. A modal study of printed PLA-CF arms backs the mechanism: thickness explained 85% of natural-frequency variation and infill 1.4% ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44245-026-00374-w)). So printed arms are not the problem. The centre body, motor bosses, screw joints and the Z axis are, and those are why whole printed frames still come out heavier than a plate. Copying a plate shape in plastic is the worst case: identical geometry in PETG deflected 64 times more than in carbon in one FEA study ([MDPI Drones](https://www.mdpi.com/2504-446X/9/11/789)).

## A bare carbon plate is already about 5% of the craft

The stock Bee35 kit is listed at 138 g with a 3.5 mm arm plate, and SpeedyBee publishes no plate-only mass and no DXF or STEP ([SpeedyBee](https://www.speedybee.com/speedybee-bee35-3-5-inch-frame/)). The researchers estimate your remaining arm plate at **24 to 32 g** from an assumed 45 to 60 cm2 of area. Your scale replaces that guess in ten seconds.

A purpose-drawn X plate at 150 mm wheelbase with a 40 x 40 mm centre and 10 mm arms works out to about **17 g at 2.5 mm, 20 g at 3 mm and 24 g at 3.5 mm** (researchers' estimate at 1.5 g/cm3, plus or minus 7%). The HyperLite UL3, a 3 mm unibody at 131 mm, is listed at 14.5 g, which cross-checks ([Pyrodrone](https://pyrodrone.com/collections/3-inch-frames/products/hyperlite-ul3-toothpick-frame)). Frames sold for 2004 motors use 3 to 4 mm arms, but they are sized for crashes you don't need to survive. One builder found 2.5 mm "plenty strong" at 3.5 inch, though on a 99 g craft ([racer.lt](https://racer.lt/build/rotorbuilds-picklepick-35-hdz-lite-the-3-5-inch-hdzero-digital-ultralight-toothpick-65)). A 2 mm single plate under 2004 motors is outside anything any listed frame does.

The limit on thinning is stiffness, not strength. The researchers' cantilever estimate (12 mm wide arm, 50 mm free length, assumed 20 g motor) gives a first bending mode near 144 Hz at 2.5 mm and 189 Hz at 3 mm, against an estimated hover motor frequency of 290 to 400 Hz (assumed thrust coefficient, not measured). All of these sit below the motor band, so the job is to keep the arm mode from landing on it, and PX4's default 30 Hz gyro cutoff tolerates a softer frame than a Betaflight tune would ([PX4 filter tuning](https://docs.px4.io/main/en/config_mc/filter_tuning)). One design rule falls out of the same formula (my arithmetic): stiffness goes with thickness cubed but only linearly with width, so a 3 x 10 mm arm is about 1.4 times stiffer than a 2.5 x 12 mm arm at the same mass. Go thicker and narrower, not thinner and wider. That also means you could simply narrow the arms of the Bee35 plate you already have.

Cutting is cheap. SendCutSend waterjets 2, 3 and 4 mm carbon in 2 to 4 days with instant pricing, and CNCMadness has no minimum order ([SendCutSend](https://sendcutsend.com/?p=5644), [CNCMadness](https://www.cncmadness.com/)). No current price for a single small plate was obtained; the researchers guess $20 to $50 before shipping. Cutting it yourself works on a desktop CNC, but carbon dust is conductive and a lung irritant, so cut wet and wear a P100 ([Easy Composites](https://www.easycomposites.co.uk/learning/cutting-carbon-fibre-sheet-on-a-desktop-cnc)).

## Rod hybrids only pay off when the cage carries the motors

Carbon tube is very light: four 60 mm arms weigh about 6 g in 6/4 mm tube and 8 g in 8/6 mm (estimate from [R&G](https://www.r-g.de/en/art/730302) per-metre masses). But motor mounts, clamps and the centre body dominate at this size, which is why every commercial 3 to 3.5 inch frame is flat plate. The only well-documented small tube frame, Feather 3, is 5 g on a sub-30 g craft and uses square tube so the motor mounts can't rotate ([MakerWorld](https://makerworld.com/models/1424214)). Builders using round tube report twist, front-to-back flex, and the printed motor mount as the soft link ([IntoFPV](https://intofpv.com/t-cf-tube-3d-print-frame?pid=245892)). No source publishes a frame mass or a stiffness number for a round-tube or rod-arm quad between 100 and 250 mm. For 2004 motors I'd guess 18 to 25 g for tubes, four printed mounts and a printed centre (my estimate, unsupported by any weighed build), which does not beat a 2.5 to 3 mm plate.

The case for the hybrid is the cage. The best published merged structures put airframe plus protection at about 10% of all-up mass (RMF-Owl, 145 g of 1.45 kg, [arXiv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2202.11055)), against roughly 20 to 25% for a separate frame and cage. At 400 g that is a 40 to 60 g target for frame and cage together. It is an aggressive target: nothing published in the 250 to 700 g class is under about 15%, and 3 mm rod and printable node sizes don't shrink with the vehicle. Your rod-in-node method is the same construction as CogniFly, a 241 g quad whose 3 mm rod exoskeleton is the whole airframe, but CogniFly is deliberately soft for impacts, not a stiff motor platform ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.04423)). If you try it, each motor needs at least two non-collinear rods (single round rods twist), carbon or metal under the motor rather than bare PLA-CF, and compliance only between cage and core.

| Option | Frame mass, no cage | Basis | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Keep cut-down Bee35 plate (3.5 mm) | 24 to 32 g | Estimate, unweighed | None; heaviest carbon option |
| Custom plate, 2.5 to 3 mm | 17 to 20 g | Researchers' estimate | Arm mode drops toward 150 Hz |
| Full print, PLA-CF or PETG-CF | 35 to 50 g | Estimate scaled from 1404-class frames | Heat at motor mounts, weak Z, soft joints |
| Printed centre + carbon tubes or rods | 18 to 25 g | My estimate, no weighed example | Twist, soft printed motor mounts, unproven |
| Rod truss merged with cage | 40 to 60 g frame and cage together | Target from 1 to 1.5 kg designs | Not demonstrated at this size |

## Conclusion

Recommendation: buy or cut a lighter carbon plate. Draw a 3 mm X plate with narrow arms (about 20 g, estimate), with a 2.5 mm version as the stretch (about 17 g, estimate), and put the cage rod hard points on it so printed parts stay as nodes and the FC mount only. Keep flying the Bee35 plate until then. Skip the full printed frame. Treat the rod-truss cage-as-frame as a side experiment, because it is the only path to tens of grams rather than single digits, and also the only one with no published proof.

Five measurements settle it. Weigh the cut Bee35 plate, the FC mount and the current cage; if the plate is under about 22 g, a new plate isn't worth the order, and the cage weight tells you where the real grams are. Upload a DXF to SendCutSend and CNCMadness for an actual price. Log a hover on the current plate with ESC RPM telemetry and read the PX4 actuator-controls FFT and acceleration plots: that gives the real hover motor frequency, the current arm mode and your margin for thinning. Tape a thermocouple to a motor base after a full-pack hover; under about 45 C opens PLA-CF near the motors, above it means PETG-CF or carbon only. For any hybrid arm, clamp the centre, hang a known weight at the motor position, and compare deflection and twist against the plate before it ever flies, then repeat the hover log on the same electronics. That last log would also be the first printed-vs-carbon flight comparison at this size that anyone has recorded.
