# Lantern cage needs tougher nodes, not more rods

The Nox Scout v10 "lantern" already follows the field's geometric rules (rod bow at break smaller than prop clearance, spans capped at 100 mm, a smooth convex shell doing the dock-centring work, a square funnel that clocks yaw) and its ~111 g (E) absolute cage mass sits exactly where the two best-known commercial cages land (Elios 1 ~100 g, Flybotix ASIO <100 g). What it lacks is everything the field uses to turn a rod cage into a collision-tolerant one: compliance between cage and core, tough node polymer, a sleeve on the rods, and a sacrificial joint that lets a damaged panel unplug instead of a rod snapping. Its declared design hit of 0.5 J at 1.5 m/s (E) is the lowest of any built benchmark except a 25 g pico quad; EPFL's Gimball, the closest rigid-polyhedron precedent, survived 3 m/s (1.7 J) with ABS nodes, and every Elios generation is rated for roughly 5 to 8 J. The 25 % mass fraction (E) is high against the 8 to 22 % band for rigid cages, but that is the price of a 450 g airframe carrying a 100 g-class cage, not evidence of a fat cage. The evidence points to a short, cheap change list: print the 27 nodes in PA-CF or PC instead of PETG (1.3 to 2x the notched impact energy, ~17 % lighter nodes), measure the actual rod batch before trusting the 0.5 J figure (hobby pultrusions test at 85 to 113 GPa, not the 131 GPa the figure implies), sleeve the two drum rings, convert the mid-span tees to clip joints, and flash reversible ESC firmware as zero-mass insurance. A six-chip ToF ring costs 10 to 15 g and is only worth it if the Scout ever flies outside the Overseer's view; the field's own leader, Flyability, chose cage over sensors and still does.

## Commercial cages converge on ~100 g, spherical or lantern, always on compliant mounts

The commercial market is essentially two companies plus add-ons. Flyability's Elios line is the descendant of EPFL's Gimball: a spherical polyhedron of CFRP rods joined by 3D-printed reinforced-plastic nodes into pentagon and hexagon modules tied together so a damaged section swaps in the field, developed over "five years and seven design generations" ([UST Magazine 8](https://www.ust-media.com/ust-magazine/UST008/43/)). The Elios 1 cage was **120 rods in 60 pentagonal bindings, ~100 g, under 400 mm diameter** on a **700 g** drone, and was rated to **15 km/h (4.2 m/s)** wall hits ([UST 8](https://www.ust-media.com/ust-magazine/UST008/files/assets/basic-html/page-43.html); [IKM datasheet](https://www.ikm.no/getfile.php/1385906-1591746760/Import/new-equipment-catalog/WUAV/439389.pdf)). That 400 mm number was not aesthetic; the datasheet cites DN400 pipe access. Thévoz described the design principle plainly: carbon is "amazingly strong in traction, but weak when bent", so the cage is arranged so that members work in tension ([DroneBelow interview](https://dronebelow.com/2018/03/29/safe-drones-for-inaccessible-places-interview-with-patrick-thevoz-ceo-flyability/)).

Elios 2 (2019) and Elios 3 (2022) dropped the Elios 1 gimbal for a fixed cage and moved the collision tolerance into "thermoplastic elastomer suspensions" between core and cage plus fast-reversing motors and a collision-aware controller ([Elios 2 spec](https://mfe-is.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/MFE-Rentals-Elios-2.pdf); [Elios 3 spec](https://nexxis.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2022/06/20220519_E3_Tech_Spec_web_version.pdf)). Elios 2 is rated **3 m/s on flat objects and 1.5 m/s on sharp objects** at under 1450 g; Elios 3 at 1900 g is rated **≥100 hits without damage below 2 m/s frontal and 2.4 m/s from any direction**, structural damage under 3 m/s, and "full-speed collisions in Attitude and Sport modes will destroy the drone" ([Elios 3 spec](https://nexxis.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2022/06/20220519_E3_Tech_Spec_web_version.pdf)). Converting to energy with the published masses gives roughly **6 J (Elios 1), 6.5 J flat / 1.6 J sharp (Elios 2), 5.5 J no-damage / 8.5 J damage-onset (Elios 3)** (C): the rated speed fell with each generation as mass tripled, but the energy per hit stayed around 5 to 8 J, which says the cage structure's energy capacity, not speed, is the real limit. Flyability's own beam patent sets a **target collision energy of 1 to 10 J**, wraps the UD carbon core in a tough "stress distribution layer" of TPE, PU or similar at **5 to 15 % of core diameter** claimed to raise beam strain energy by **95 % to over 300 %**, and makes corner connectors release at **70 to 90 % of the beam's rupture strain** so modules unplug before rods break; its suspension beams are 100 to 400 MPa-modulus polymer with a **damping ratio of 0.4 to 0.6** ([Flyability US 2020/0189712](https://patents.justia.com/patent/20200189712)). Nobody publishes an Elios cage mass, rod diameter or node count; the one reseller figure of 620 g is almost certainly a listing error ([XBOOM listing](https://xboom.in/shop/drones/enterprise-drone/indoor-inspection-drones/elios-3)).

Flybotix ASIO is the other reference and is the lantern's closest commercial cousin: **10 longitudinal polymer-composite spars meeting at polar rings, strings criss-crossed between them, just under 100 g on a ~1 kg coaxial drone, 395 x 295 mm**, designed to crumple slightly inward rather than bounce, swapped in minutes, and drop-tested from 1.5 m undamaged ([UST Magazine 36](https://www.ust-media.com/ust-magazine/UST036/61/)). The patent specifies flat ribs **0.5 to 5 mm thick, 2 to 20 mm wide, wider at mid-span** to absorb impact, and non-elastic strings ([Flybotix US12275542](https://patents.google.com/patent/US12275542)). ASIO X holds the cage under 100 g on 1.15 kg, about **8.7 %** of take-off mass (E) ([Measur ASIO X](https://measur.ca/pages/learn/indoor-navigation-flybotix-asio-x)). The add-on cages at the bottom of the market show what not to do: Heliguy's 230 g Mavic 2 cage is **17 % of MTOW**, halves flight time, and Flyability's taxonomy calls such rigidly bolted add-ons "not collision tolerant" because a bump moves the drone ([Heliguy](https://www.heliguy.com/blogs/posts/heliguy-unveils-dji-mavic-2-drone-cage); [Flyability cage guide](https://www.flyability.com/blog/drone-cage)). The pattern across every fixed-cage product is the same: compliance lives somewhere between cage and core (TPE pods on Elios, crumpling spars on ASIO, a deforming cage on Skypersonic), and a rigid cage bolted straight to the frame appears only on cheap add-ons.

## Research cages win on protection per gram by buckling rods and softening the core

The research literature supplies the numbers the vendors withhold. Gimball (EPFL, 2014) was **385 g total with an 84 g cage (22 %) and a 56 g gimbal**, a spherical polyhedron of radius 17 cm built from pultruded carbon rods in 3D-printed ABS connectors, and it survived "numerous collisions at speeds up to 3 m/s"; above that, the **3D-printed cage parts or gimbal joints broke**, never the rods ([Briod et al. 2014](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/190790/files/collision_resilient_flying_robots_LIS2014.pdf)). EPFL's AirBurr Euler-spring layout is the protection-per-gram record for a rigid-rod design: **24 pultruded 2 mm x 200 mm carbon rods in 8 tetrahedra, 40 g including end pieces (14 % of 282 g), each rod absorbing ~1.5 J in post-buckling**, with rod length set by the rule **L = √(8/3) × platform radius** so the hit load lands on the rod axis; of 50 wall collisions, 4 caused damage, twice from rotors flexing together and twice from nylon ties failing ([Klaptocz et al.](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/187769/files/cage-IROS.pdf?version=1)). Berkeley's icosahedron tensegrity goes further at a higher mass fraction: **95 g shell (32 %) on 300 g, 6 mm rods, survived a 7 m drop (11.7 m/s, ~20 J) and a 7.8 m/s wall hit; a 250 g prop-guard quad fractured at 3.25 m**, and in simulation at equal 50 g structural mass the tensegrity saw **34 MPa mean peak rod stress against 100 MPa for the guard** ([Zha PhD thesis](https://hiperlab.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/2023-JiamingZha_PhD.pdf); [Zha et al. 2211.12045](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2211.12045)). At the small end, Penn's 25 g pico quad carries a **2 g Gömböc-inspired carbon cage (8 %)** rated above 4 m/s, about 0.2 J (C) ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/the-secret-to-small-drone-obstacle-avoidance-is-to-just-crash-into-stuff)).

Two results matter more than any topology. Bilkent's 1200-plus collision tests across four micro-quad configurations found that **compliant bumpers and prop guards make collisions more elastic but do not improve energy dissipation, while a softer inner frame raises damping and lowers accelerations** ([Abazari et al. 2024](https://repository.bilkent.edu.tr/items/1033b7aa-8f57-4739-8f5a-b596f5078dad/full)). That is the mechanism behind Elios's TPE pods and the reason a soft rod on a rigid mount gains little. The second result is the failure hierarchy: across Gimball, AirBurr, Berkeley, EPFL's origami cargo cage (printed joints broke 3 of 5 times, glue twice, at 297 N) and the Yale tensegrity drop model (printed end caps are the next weakest link after the rods), **what breaks is the printed node, the tie or the glue line; pultruded carbon almost never fails first** ([Kornatowski et al.](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/277795/files/09091043.pdf?version=1); [Cimatti et al. 2026](https://www.eng.yale.edu/faboratory/publications/conference/2026/Cimatti%20-%202026%20-%20Modeling%20the%20critical%20drop%20height%20of%20tensegrity%20robots.pdf)). The survey of collision-tolerant aerial robots lists the shapes (sphere, cube, tetrahedral Euler springs, icosahedral tensegrity, per-prop shells) and explicitly does not rank them ([Survey 2212.03196](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.03196)); no clean equal-mass bake-off of sphere versus cylinder versus polyhedron exists.

Materials data explains why the node is the weak link and how much a material change buys. Premium pultruded UD carbon rod is quoted at **1.72 GPa tensile, 1.83 GPa flexural, 131 to 138 GPa modulus, 1.5 g/cm³** ([Rock West](https://www.rockwestcomposites.com/47316-4.html)), but EPFL measured its 2 mm rods at **113 ± 15 GPa** and Bristol measured pultruded rod at **85 to 88 GPa with 1.34 % compressive strain to failure** ([Klaptocz et al.](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/187769/files/cage-IROS.pdf?version=1); [Univ. of Bristol](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/the-experimental-investigation-of-the-compressive-performance-of-/)), and Easy Composites quotes its own 3 mm T300 rod at only **250 to 400 MPa flexural, 20 to 30 GPa modulus, 11 g/m** ([Easy Composites TDS](https://media.easycomposites.eu/datasheets/EC-TDS-Carbon-Fibre-Pultrusions.pdf); [3 mm rod page](https://www.easycomposites.eu/3mm-carbon-fibre-rod)). Elastic energy to failure scales as σ²/2E, which is **~12.8 MJ/m³ on the premium numbers and ~2.7 MJ/m³ on the hobby datasheet**, a factor of nearly five (E). Fibreglass rod of the same diameter is ~32 % heavier and 3x more compliant but reaches 2.5 to 3 % strain, so it stores 2 to 3x the energy per unit stiffness and bends to a ~50 to 60 mm radius instead of ~115 mm (E from [Glasforms](https://electronics.polyone.com/es/node/10713) and [R&G](https://r-g.de/en/art/610001)). For printed nodes, the datasheets put **PETG at ~6 kJ/m² notched Charpy** ([Prusament PETG](https://filament2print.com/en/pet-petg-cpe/3427-prusament-petg-.html)) against **PA12-CF at 9.9 kJ/m² notched at 1.06 g/cm³** ([Polymaker PA12-CF](https://polymaker.com/wp-content/uploads/lana-downloads/PolyMide_PA12_CF_TDS_V5.4.pdf)), and on Bambu's XY impact table **PETG HF 31.5, PC 34.8, ABS 39.3, PA6-CF 40.3, PAHT-CF 57.5, TPU 95A 123 kJ/m²** ([Bambu filament guide](https://www.lesimprimantes3d.fr/dl/Bambu_Lab_Filament-guide.pdf)). The honest reading is a 1.3 to 2x toughness gain from carbon-nylon over PETG, not the order of magnitude TPU shows, plus the practical field evidence that PLA shatters, PETG cracks on direct hits, and PC guards at 0.9 g survived "quite a lot" of wall crashes ([uavmodel guards guide](https://blog.uavmodel.com/3d-printed-propeller-guards-design-print-and-safety-guide/); [incoherency.co.uk](https://incoherency.co.uk/blog/stories/rotor-guards.html)). The node mass benchmark from AirBurr is roughly **0.7 g per rod** of printed end piece plus tie (E, 17 g of non-carbon mass across 24 rods).

## Docks, sensing and flip recovery: the field offers rules, not caged precedents

No commercial caged inspection drone has an automated dock; Elios and ASIO are hand-launched and ScoutDI uses a tether ([Measur guide](https://measur.ca/blogs/news/guide-to-the-top-2-confined-space-drones-scout-137-drone-system-and-flybotix-asio-x)). The dock literature therefore comes from uncaged drones but gives usable rules. Passive slide-down docks need **slope α > arctan μ**; a printed PETG pyramid at **12.7°** worked for a 700 g wireless charger, and the funnel-per-foot patent quotes **~2.3° for PTFE at μ = 0.04** against ~31° for steel on aluminium, warning that friction must stay low enough that the vehicle does not stop part way ([arXiv 2309.05433](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.05433); [US10434885](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10434885)). Skydio's dock lands the drone on its lowest convex surface (the battery underside) in a funnel whose base corners stop rotation and targets a **~5 x 5 cm landing from a 5 cm hover** ([Skydio US11873116](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11873116)); the self-orienting funnel patent uses a rounded-square cross-section with corner grooves to clock a quad's yaw, names "propeller enclosures" as the drone-side sliding feature, and falls back to a yaw command if the drone stalls on the slope ([US12391415](https://patents.google.com/patent/US12391415)). Deep V-shaped pads produced ground-effect "blowback" in the arXiv study, fixed by reorienting the tip ([arXiv 2309.05433](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.05433)). DJI's Dock 2 moved from pusher rods to a sloped "nest" that physically guides the drone in, with ±0.1 m RTK hover accuracy and a 3° tilt limit ([DJI Dock 2 specs](https://enterprise.dji.com/dock-2/specs)).

On sensing, the field's leader made the choice the Scout is making. Flyability, rather than "creating and testing sensors that can scan their surrounding area to avoid bumping", designed a protective frame instead ([Women Who Drone](https://www.womenwhodrone.co/post/2019/06/19/cage-the-drone-flyabilitys-design-for-collision-tolerant-drones-improves-indoor-inspectio)); Elios 3 carries **one ToF plus three vision cameras and a lidar for SLAM**, used for position hold and a 30 to 200 cm "distance lock", not stop-before-contact ([Elios 3 spec](https://nexxis.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2022/06/20220519_E3_Tech_Spec_web_version.pdf); [Elios 2 brochure](https://www.xboom.in/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/Copy-of-Eliso-2_compressed-1.pdf)). Penn's GRASP lab went further: "the penalty due to collisions is small at these scales", so its controller ignores obstacles entirely ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/the-secret-to-small-drone-obstacle-avoidance-is-to-just-crash-into-stuff)). The survey frames tolerance as "a last-resort mechanism" usually paired with avoidance ([Survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.03196)). If sensing is added, the lightest full-azimuth option is a ring of six **VL53L5CX** multizone chips (45° x 45° FoV, 4 m dark but **1.1 to 1.7 m at 5 klux**, 95 to 156 mW each in autonomous 10 Hz mode) at an estimated **10 to 15 g** with harness; the lightest spinning scanner, the LD19, is **47 g and 0.9 W** ([VL53L5CX datasheet](https://cdn.sparkfun.com/assets/6/e/3/0/6/vl53l5cx-datasheet.pdf); [Waveshare LD19](https://www.waveshare.com/Triangulation-LiDAR-LD14.htm)). Sensors inside a cage see the cage: Terabee's rule is nothing within ±15° of each optical axis and no reflective object within 1 m inside ±45°, so emitters must sit in cage windows or struts must be masked per zone ([Tower Evo manual](https://terabee.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/TeraRanger-Tower-Evo-user-manual.pdf)).

Flip recovery splits three ways. Elios 3 rights itself in the air by reversing the propellers distal from the contact point, with thrust slewing at **8 to 50x hover thrust per second** ([Flyability US12134488](https://patents.google.com/patent/US12134488)). ASIO rights itself passively "inspired by turtles", rocking like a pendulum for a few seconds ([EPFL news](https://actu.epfl.ch/news/new-turtle-like-drone-can-fly-twice-as-long)), and the geometric rule for passive righting is that the CoM projection falls outside the support polygon of every face except the intended one, which a Michigan hexapod shell achieved from all 28 faces ([King & Revzen ICRA 2024](https://www.birds.eecs.umich.edu/assets/pdf/2024-King-Revzen-ICRA.pdf)). Consumer FPV uses turtle mode, which needs only DShot ESCs with reversal-capable firmware (BLHeli_32, AM32, Bluejay; stock BLHeli_S lacks it), fails when props are obstructed or the surface is slick, and risks ESC overheating in sustained reverse ([ArduPilot Turtle Mode](https://ardupilot.org/copter/docs/turtle-mode.html); [Oscar Liang](https://oscarliang.com/setup-turtle-mode-flip-over-after-crash/)). No source tests turtle mode on a fully caged quad.

## How the lantern lines up against each benchmark

The lantern's own numbers, restated for the table: 41 rods of 3.0 mm pultruded carbon, 3.78 m, 42 g; 27 PETG nodes ~61 g; ~111 g total, ~25 % of ~450 g (E); 224 mm wide x 132 mm tall (C); closest rod 12.5 mm from the swept props (C); longest free span 100 mm (C); rod break energy 5.1 N·mm per mm of span, so 0.5 J at 100 mm with 6.4 mm bow and 1.0 J at 200 mm with 25 mm bow (E); design hit 0.5 J, drone into a static plank at 1.5 m/s (E). Node mass per rod end is 61 g / 82 ends ≈ **0.74 g** (C), inside the ~0.7 to 1 g benchmark, but nodes are **55 % of cage mass** (C) against ~43 % in AirBurr.

Read across the rows, the lantern is tightest on envelope by a wide margin: at 224 mm wide around a 197 mm prop span (C) it is only **14 % larger than the props**, where ARQ's cage is 55 % larger ([Liu & Karydis](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.02061)) and Gimball wraps a 34 cm sphere around a frame that would fit in far less. That tightness is what the 45° funnel and the swap box want, and it is also what forces 100 mm spans and the 0.5 J ceiling: the lantern's own numbers say a 200 mm span would store twice the energy but bow 25 mm, double the 12.5 mm clearance. The field resolves the same tension either by pushing rods further out (AirBurr's 20 mm margin, the origami cage's 35 mm with a "deflection under 30 N must stay inside the clearance" check, [Kornatowski et al.](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/277795/files/09091043.pdf?version=1)) or by letting the whole cage move on compliant mounts so the rods do not have to bow at all.

## Verdict: keep the geometry, fix the nodes, test the rods, decide on compliance deliberately

Where the lantern matches or beats the field. The bow-smaller-than-clearance rule is satisfied with margin (6.4 mm versus 12.5 mm, C/E), which is more than Berkeley, Gimball or any Elios publishes. The crown sits upstream of the props, where EPFL measured a dense 7 % grid costing only 0.6 % lift versus 6.3 % downstream ([Kornatowski et al.](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/277795/files/09091043.pdf?version=1)), so eight spokes above the disc are close to free aerodynamically. The dock concept is ahead of every caged product, none of which docks at all, and it follows the uncaged patents' best ideas: the cage's convex shell is the sliding contact (the clocking-groove patent names prop enclosures for exactly this), the square funnel clocks yaw the way the rounded-square funnel does, and the 45° face angle clears the α > arctan μ rule by a factor of four even at μ = 0.2 (arctan 0.2 = 11.3°; C). The settle sim's μ ≤ 0.2 ceiling is stricter than the single-surface rule because eight fixed pads load simultaneously, which is a multi-contact wedging problem the funnel-per-foot patent sidesteps by preferring **3 or 4 feet** ([US10434885](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10434885)); the UHMW tape is the right family of fix (PTFE-class liners are what the patent assumes), and lining the funnel faces as well as the pads is cheap insurance. One practical check costs nothing: tilt a taped pad on a bare PETG face until it slides; the angle is arctan μ, and it either clears 11.3° or it does not. The absolute cage mass of ~111 g is also not out of line: a 3 mm-rod sphere around a 450 g quad lands near **100 to 120 g** once nodes are counted (E), which is where Elios 1 and ASIO both sit, so the 25 % fraction is the airframe being light, not the cage being fat.

Where it falls short. First, the design hit. 0.5 J at 1.5 m/s is the Elios 2 "sharp object" speed and half the field's flat-wall speed; matching Gimball's 3 m/s with a 450 g drone means **2.0 J** (C), four rods' worth at 100 mm spans with nothing left for the node. Worse, the 0.5 J figure assumes premium rod properties: back-calculating a simply supported 3 mm rod at 100 mm span bowing 6.4 mm gives a surface strain near 1.15 % and a stored energy of 0.5 J only if E ≈ 130 GPa; at the 85 to 113 GPa that EPFL and Bristol actually measured it is **~0.3 to 0.4 J**, and on Easy Composites' own 400 MPa / 30 GPa datasheet it is a fifth of that (E, from [Klaptocz et al.](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/187769/files/cage-IROS.pdf?version=1), [Bristol](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/the-experimental-investigation-of-the-compressive-performance-of-/), [Easy Composites TDS](https://media.easycomposites.eu/datasheets/EC-TDS-Carbon-Fibre-Pultrusions.pdf)). A three-point bend of three rods from the actual batch to failure is the single highest-value test in this report. Second, the node material: every benchmark that failed, failed at the printed node, and PETG is the weakest common choice on the notched data. Third, no compliance anywhere between cage and core, which every fixed-cage commercial product has and which Bilkent shows is where the damping has to live. Fourth, no sleeve, so the sharp-edge penalty Flyability rates at a factor of two applies in full. Fifth, no sacrificial joint: epoxied sockets mean a hard hit breaks a rod or a node, and nothing unplugs; Elios sells pentagons as consumables and ASIO swaps the cage in five minutes. Sixth, the sled: its 90 mm rods sit 2.5 mm under the battery (C) but a 90 mm span bows roughly 5 mm before breaking (E, scaled from the 6.4 mm at 100 mm), so a hard bottom landing drives the sled into the cartridge before the rod yields; Skydio lands on the battery underside on purpose, so this is survivable if the cartridge shell is the load path, but it should be a decision, not an accident. Seventh, the flat hub top is a deliberately stable inverted rest face, the opposite of the Michigan and Gömböc rule, so passive righting is off the table and the whole recovery rides on an unmeasured motor-thrust estimate.

Two things not to change. The 100 mm span cap and 12.5 mm clearance are the right pair for a cage this close to the props; stretching spans for energy without widening the drone would put the bow through the disc, and widening the drone breaks the funnel and box. And the drum-plus-crown topology is defensible: it is ASIO's lantern with rings instead of strings, nobody has shown a sphere beats it at equal mass, and the flat hub that spoils passive righting is also the only reason the drone can rest inverted without the props touching anything.

## Conclusion

The evidence reframes the lantern's problem. It is not under-built in rods and it is not over-built in mass; it is built from the one material combination (rigid PETG nodes, bare carbon, epoxy, no suspension) that the field has consistently moved away from, and its headline energy number rests on a rod property nobody has measured. Three cheap moves, tougher and lighter nodes, a bend test, and a sleeve on the two rings that take every side hit, probably lift the real design point from "0.3 to 0.5 J if the rods are good" toward the ~1.5 to 2 J that would put a 450 g Scout at Gimball-class 3 m/s tolerance, and they do it while taking mass out, not adding it.

The genuinely open question is compliance. Everything in the literature says put elastomer between cage and core, and everything in the lantern's job (eight fixed pads, a swap head that pulls the battery straight down through the sled) says the cage must not move relative to the core at the dock. No benchmark has faced that conflict because no caged drone docks. The settle sim is the right tool to resolve it: model a few millimetres of mount travel and see whether the pads still find the funnel. If they do, the lantern gets the field's biggest single survivability gain for a few grams of TPU; if they do not, the rigid choice is justified and the toughness has to live in the nodes, which is where the rest of this report already puts it.
